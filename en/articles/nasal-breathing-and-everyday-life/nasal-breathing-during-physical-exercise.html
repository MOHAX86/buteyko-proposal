Nasal breathing during physical exercise

Author: Alik Mullakhmetov, VEDB senior instructor and examiner, more than 30 years of practicing the Buteyko method

Updated: November 25, 2026

In short. Nasal inhalation is a convenient guide to moderate exertion, but not a mandatory condition for all kinds of work. During heavy exertion, bringing in the mouth may be physiological.

The requirement to always breathe only through the nose is often presented as a universal rule. However, during physical exercise the need for ventilation changes. What is possible on a walk may not be enough during fast running, heavy strength work or a sports bout.

What happens to breathing during movement

Working muscles consume more oxygen and produce more carbon dioxide. In response, the rate and depth of breathing, minute ventilation and circulation increase.

Such an increase in breathing is physiological if it matches the intensity of the work being done. It is a mistake to judge breathing during exertion by the same outward signs as breathing at rest.

Nasal inhalation as a guide to intensity

During light and moderate exertion, inhalation can often be kept through the nose. If this requires sharply reducing the volume of inhalation, enduring severe suffocation or tensing the whole body, the chosen intensity may be too high for this way of breathing.

A practical approach:

  • during light exertion, both inhalation and exhalation can go through the nose;
  • during moderate exertion, it is desirable to keep inhaling through the nose, and to exhale through pursed lips if necessary;
  • during heavy exertion, bringing in the mouth may be physiological.

At the same time, the limits depend on fitness, the condition of the airways and the nature of the exercise.

Why the mouth must not be kept closed at any cost

Trying to breathe only through the nose no matter what may lead to excessive breath holds, a breakdown in movement technique and sharp muscle tension.

Prolonged straining with a closed glottis during heavy strength work is especially dangerous. It can cause considerable fluctuations in intrathoracic pressure and blood pressure.

The Buteyko method does not require reducing ventilation below the body’s physiological need. The aim is to eliminate excessive breathing, not to fight the normal response to exertion.

How to increase the load gradually

Start at a level that allows you to keep your breathing controlled and your movement technique correct. Increase the intensity gradually, observing:

  • the ability to inhale through the nose;
  • the emergence of a need to open your mouth;
  • the ability to speak during movement;
  • the severity of breathlessness;
  • the speed at which breathing recovers;
  • pulse and general well-being.

At moderate intensity, a person is usually able to talk, although singing is already difficult. At high intensity, speech has to be interrupted after a few words. This “talk test” is used as a simple guide to the relative intensity of exercise.

When exercise must be stopped

You must stop if there is pain or pressure in the chest, near-fainting, impaired coordination, unusually strong breathlessness, a sharp worsening of well-being or the appearance of symptoms that were not there before.

With cardiovascular, lung and other chronic diseases, the permissible load should be agreed with a doctor or a physical therapy specialist.

The main thing

Nasal breathing is convenient to use as an indicator of moderate intensity and as a way to better control the breathing pattern. But during heavy exertion, breathing through the mouth may be a normal part of the ventilatory response. Safety, movement technique and matching the load to the body’s capabilities are more important than the formal requirement to keep the mouth closed.

This material is for information and does not replace a doctor’s consultation and treatment. Do not cancel or change medicines and their doses on your own. Chest pain, pronounced difficulty breathing or loss of consciousness require emergency help. Breath holds must not be performed in water, while driving or where fainting is dangerous.
