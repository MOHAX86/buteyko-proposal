Nasal breathing while walking

Author: Alik Mullakhmetov, VEDB senior instructor and examiner, more than 30 years of practicing the Buteyko method

Updated: November 25, 2026

In short. During walking, breathing naturally intensifies, and inhaling through the nose helps to choose a manageable pace. This is a guide, not a strict ban. If breathing has become heavy, slow down and, if necessary, stop.

During walking, the muscles need more energy. Carbon dioxide production and the need for oxygen grow, so lung ventilation naturally increases. This is a normal response, not a sign of incorrect breathing.

During calm and moderate walking, many people can keep inhaling through the nose. This guide helps to choose a load that matches the body’s current capabilities.

Why breathing intensifies during walking

The faster a person moves, the more actively the muscles work. The respiratory center receives signals about the change in metabolism and increases ventilation: breaths may become deeper and more frequent.

In the Buteyko method, what is considered excessive is not just any increase in breathing, but ventilation that does not match the current metabolic need. So a person cannot be required to breathe during movement exactly the same way as at complete rest.

How to choose a suitable pace

To begin with, it is better to choose a speed at which you can:

  • inhale through the nose without marked strain;
  • keep a relatively calm rhythm;
  • not raise your shoulders with every inhalation;
  • not experience a severe feeling of air shortage;
  • speak in short phrases without strong breathlessness.

If you feel an urge to open your mouth wide, you can slow the pace a little. Sometimes it is enough to switch to a slower step, relax your shoulders and let your breathing recover.

Here, nasal inhalation serves as a practical guide, not a strict ban. If a person cannot breathe through the nose even when walking slowly, the patency of the nasal passages, the state of health and the baseline exercise tolerance need to be assessed.

Do you need to count steps on the inhalation and exhalation

There is no universal ratio of steps to breathing movements. It depends on speed, terrain, age, state of health and level of physical fitness.

Constant counting may be distracting and create unnecessary tension. It is more important to observe whether breathing remains manageable, whether there are any sharp, convulsive inhalations and whether it settles quickly after you slow down.

Special breathing and breath-hold patterns may be used in training, but their load should be selected by an instructor, taking the person’s condition into account.

What to do if your breathing gets out of rhythm

If breathing has become excessively heavy:

  1. reduce your speed;
  2. stop if necessary;
  3. relax your face, shoulders and abdomen;
  4. do not try to hold your breath abruptly;
  5. wait for a calm rhythm to return.

Chest pain, dizziness, near-fainting, unusual weakness or marked breathlessness require you to stop exercising and get a medical assessment.

Nasal breathing as part of daily practice

Walking makes it possible to carry the skill of calm breathing from the exercise session into ordinary life. There is no need to turn a walk into a continuous exercise. It is enough to notice from time to time whether your mouth is open, whether your breathing has sped up unnecessarily and whether any excess muscle tightness has appeared.

As exercise tolerance improves, a person can usually keep inhaling through the nose longer during faster movement. However, progress is assessed not only by this sign, but also by well-being, recovery of breathing and the overall trend of the control pause.

The main thing

Nasal breathing helps to choose a moderate pace, but it must not be maintained at any cost. The task is to move at a manageable intensity and gradually form a calmer breathing pattern.

This material is for information and does not replace a doctor’s consultation and treatment. Do not cancel or change medicines and their doses on your own. Chest pain, pronounced difficulty breathing or loss of consciousness require emergency help. Breath holds must not be performed in water, while driving or where fainting is dangerous.
