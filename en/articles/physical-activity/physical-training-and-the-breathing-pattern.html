Physical training and the breathing pattern

Author: Alik Mullakhmetov, VEDB senior instructor and examiner, more than 30 years of practicing the Buteyko method

Updated: November 25, 2026

In short. There is no single ideal rhythm: breathing changes along with the exercise. Notice unnecessary habits and do not hold back the inhalation by force for the sake of quiet breathing.

A breathing pattern is the habitual way of breathing: its rhythm, rate, depth and combination of breathing movements. In exercise this pattern changes along with the task. Walking, bending, raising the arms and holding a position do not have to be accompanied by identical breaths.

Why you do not need one ideal rhythm

People often look for a universal scheme: how many seconds to inhale, when to exhale and how many steps to take between breaths. Such a scheme seems convenient, but it does not take into account the differences between exercises and in a person’s condition.

It is especially hard to follow when the pace or body position changes. Instead of moving freely, the person starts forcing themselves to fit the count. If attention is entirely occupied by the correct second of inhalation, it is easy to miss tension or lose comfort.

What is worth observing

Pay attention to recurring habits. Is your breath held at every bend? Does an excessive preparatory breath appear before a simple movement? Do your jaws and shoulders tense up although the exercise itself is easy?

These are observations for discussion, not a self-diagnosis. A single movement of the belly or chest cannot show how well breathing matches the body’s needs. Nor is there any need to deliberately increase the movement of your belly so that your breathing looks “correct.”

How to combine technique and the Buteyko method

First a comfortable movement is mastered. Then you decide together with an instructor how to work with excessive depth of breathing and unneeded tension. If trying to watch everything at once gets in the way, the exercise is simplified.

In the methodological position of the school, an excessive inhalation is reduced primarily by relaxation and only to normal. So a motionless chest, a drawn-in belly and forcibly holding back the inhalation are not signs of good performance.

How to assess changes

Compare the same exercise under similar conditions. Has it become easier to move? Has the habit of tensing up decreased? Have any unpleasant sensations appeared? There is no need to judge the session only by how quiet your breathing looked.

An individual breathing pattern is not corrected by someone else’s video without taking your own task into account.
