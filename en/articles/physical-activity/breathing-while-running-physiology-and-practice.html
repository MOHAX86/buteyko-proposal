Breathing while running: physiology and practice

Author: Alik Mullakhmetov, VEDB senior instructor and examiner, more than 30 years of practicing the Buteyko method

Updated: November 25, 2026

In short. When running, breathing may become faster and larger in volume, and this does not yet prove hyperventilation. Choose a tolerable pace, and slow down if you feel a marked air shortage.

Running places different demands on the body than a calm walk. So the advice to “breathe as quietly as when sitting” cannot be taken as a universal rule. For health-oriented practice, it is more important to choose a tolerable load than to keep to a breathing pattern thought up in advance.

Why there is no single pattern

A light jog and an intense interval are different tasks. As effort increases, breathing may become faster and larger in volume. The change itself does not yet prove hyperventilation: what matters is whether ventilation matches the body’s current work.

There is no need either to deliberately deepen each breath or to hold back necessary breathing for the sake of outward quietness. Judging by a single sign, whether loudness, rate or the position of the mouth, oversimplifies what is happening.

How to understand the recommendation to breathe through your nose

The Buteyko school pays great attention to nasal breathing. In health jogging, it can help you notice when the chosen pace is becoming uncomfortable. But a demand to maintain it at any cost can turn the session into a struggle.

If a marked air shortage arises when you try to run with your mouth closed, reduce your speed or switch to walking. Do not try to cement the rule by willpower while keeping the same intensity. If your nose is blocked, first deal with the cause of the difficulty rather than making the exercise stricter.

What is a comfortable way to start

Those who have not run for a long time do not necessarily need to choose continuous jogging right away. You can discuss with a coach a combination of manageable intervals of walking and light running. You should not add long breath holds to them on your own.

While moving, pay attention to a habit of raising your shoulders, clenching your fists or tensing your face. Relaxing these areas should not disrupt your technique and stability. There is no need to deliberately pull in your belly or fix your chest in place.

When an individual assessment is needed

Cough, wheezing or chest tightness during or after a run are a reason to discuss the symptoms with a doctor. With asthma, one follows the prescribed action plan; mastering VEDB is not grounds for stopping medicines on your own.

Running is not a mandatory stage for every practitioner. If walking suits you better, it remains a fully valid form of movement.
