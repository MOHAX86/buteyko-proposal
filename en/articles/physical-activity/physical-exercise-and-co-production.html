Physical exercise and CO₂ production

Author: Alik Mullakhmetov, VEDB senior instructor and examiner, more than 30 years of practicing the Buteyko method

Updated: November 25, 2026

In short. During muscular work more CO₂ is produced, but this does not yet mean that it accumulates: the result also depends on ventilation. The aim of the sessions is not its maximum accumulation.

Carbon dioxide does not appear in the body only because of breath holding. It is constantly formed in metabolic processes. During muscular work its production increases, and the lungs take part in removing it. To understand the Buteyko method, it is important to distinguish between the production of the gas and its content in the body.

Production is not the same as accumulation

Imagine two processes acting at the same time: the tissues produce CO₂, and the lungs remove it. An increase in the first process does not yet mean that the concentration of the gas in the blood necessarily rises. The result also depends on ventilation.

So it is wrong to conclude that the harder the workout, the more beneficial CO₂ is bound to accumulate. It is equally wrong to think that any increase in breathing during movement necessarily means an excessive loss of it. These processes cannot be assessed separately from each other.

How this relates to Buteyko’s position

In a 1972 lecture, Buteyko emphasized the role of physical work and proposed considering breathing relative to metabolism. The meaning of this point for everyday practice is not to confuse the outwardly more noticeable breathing of a working person with excessive breathing at rest.

In the school’s current methodological framework, the goal is formulated as normalizing gas constants and reducing excessive breathing to the physiological norm. Manageable movement is included in this work together with relaxation and changes in everyday habits.

Why there is no need to deliberately retain CO₂

Understanding the role of carbon dioxide is no reason to hold your breath at every effort. Self-directed experiments with pauses to the limit add one more load to physical work and make it harder to assess your condition.

Nor is there any need to judge the benefit of a walk by how pronounced the air shortage is. Strained endurance does not show that the required concentration of the gas has been reached. How you feel and the outward look of your breathing do not replace measurements.

What is useful to observe in practice

Note which load is comfortably tolerated, whether extra tension appears and how rest goes. When working with an instructor, this information is considered together with symptoms and the trend of the control pause under comparable conditions.
