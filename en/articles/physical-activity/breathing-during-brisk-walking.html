Breathing during brisk walking

Author: Alik Mullakhmetov, VEDB senior instructor and examiner, more than 30 years of practicing the Buteyko method

Updated: November 25, 2026

In short. Increased breathing during a brisk pace is not in itself a mistake. Speed up gradually and without unnecessary tension, and slow down if you feel a marked air shortage.

Brisk walking does not begin at a particular figure on your watch. For one person a habitual energetic pace remains easy, while for another the same pace already requires considerable effort. So breathing needs to be assessed by your own condition and the conditions of the route.

What changes when you speed up

When your pace quickens, breathing is usually felt more distinctly. It is a mistake to consider any increase in it a sign of incorrect practice and to suppress the inhalation at once. The other extreme is to take big breaths in advance, as if it were impossible to speed up without them.

In the approach of the Buteyko school, it is important to remove extra tension. Speeding up does not require clenched teeth, raised shoulders or tense hands. If the movement stays easy, a person can notice these habits without trying to control the breathing cycle completely.

How to move to a faster pace

First walk part of the route at a comfortable pace. Then speed up a little and assess whether your movements remain free. Do not change everything at once: speed, length of the walk and difficulty of the path. Otherwise it is hard to understand what exactly turned out to be excessive.

On a health walk you can return to your ordinary pace long before marked fatigue. A comfortable pace today may differ from yesterday’s, for example after a stressful day or on an unfamiliar slope.

Why you should not chase someone else’s speed

On a walk together it is easy to adjust to a faster companion and miss the first signs of overload. If you have to endure a marked air shortage for the sake of keeping up nasal breathing, it is wiser to slow down. Explaining your pace to your companion is more useful than proving that you can keep up.

Walking uphill, a heavy bag and haste are considered separately. Formally the speed may stay the same, but the task itself becomes harder. On such a stretch you can shorten your stride and take a pause.

What counts as a good result

Not a speed record, but the ability to move without a constant struggle with your breathing. After the walk it is useful to note where you stayed comfortable and when you had to slow down. These observations help to gradually choose suitable activity together with an instructor.

If unusual symptoms appear or a habitual load is suddenly tolerated noticeably worse, do not explain everything by breathing technique alone. The cause needs to be assessed.
