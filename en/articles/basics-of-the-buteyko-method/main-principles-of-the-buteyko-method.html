Main principles of the Buteyko method

Author: Alik Mullakhmetov, VEDB senior instructor and examiner, more than 30 years of practicing the Buteyko method

Updated: November 25, 2026

In short. The method rests on conscious observation, relaxation, gradualness, moderation, regularity and monitoring of changes over time. Together they help reduce excess ventilation to the physiological norm.

The Buteyko method is not built around a single technique: it is consistent work in which the principles complement one another.

Why the method cannot be reduced to one exercise

A single exercise lasts a limited time, while the breathing habit shows itself all day long. It is influenced by the person’s condition and by factors that deepen breathing: stress, physical inactivity, poor posture, excessive emotions, alarming content, improper physical exercise and other influences.

Therefore the goal is not the mechanical repetition of a technique, but the step-by-step normalization of breathing and gas parameters. Exercises serve this goal, but they do not replace an understanding of the method.

The principle of conscious observation of breathing

First, a person learns to notice how they breathe at rest and in different circumstances. It is important to see unnecessary effort, a deeper inhalation, a change in rate, and tension in the face, shoulders, chest or abdomen.

Observation does not mean constant anxious checking of every breath. It is a calm skill of recognizing changes and understanding what exactly may have caused them.

The principle of relaxation

The depth of inhalation is reduced primarily through relaxation, and the air shortage remains slight. If a person tries to hold back their breathing by force, the tension itself becomes an obstacle. Relaxation concerns not only the respiratory muscles but also the general state of the body.

This principle distinguishes the normalization of breathing from forcible suppression. A person does not forbid themselves to breathe, but gradually removes unnecessary effort.

The principle of gradually reducing excess ventilation

Ventilation is reduced in stages and only to the physiological norm. The method does not aim for the slowest or smallest possible breathing. The changes should be manageable, so that the new pattern becomes established without sharp recovery breathing.

The principle of moderation: no records and no forcing

Strenuous endurance, marked suffocation and the desire to increase the breath hold at any cost are not indicators of good practice. An excessive load can worsen how you feel and reinforce an incorrect way of performing.

The work should remain manageable. If your condition worsens, symptoms intensify or marked discomfort arises, the exercise is stopped and breathing is allowed to calm down.

The principle of regularity

The breathing habit does not change after one successful session. What is needed is regular practice according to the schedule you have received, homework and returning to the skill in everyday situations. Regularity is more important than one-off records.

The principle of monitoring changes over time

The school uses three main criteria: changes in the control pause, changes in symptoms and general well-being. After a correctly performed exercise, the CP is expected to increase by about 5 seconds or more; a gain of 5–10 seconds is often considered. With regular practice, the benchmark for long-term progress is about 1–2 seconds per week.

The number is not assessed separately from your condition. Symptoms should become milder and well-being should improve. Worsening calls for a check of the technique and the load.

How the principles work together

Observation helps you see the excess. Relaxation allows you to reduce it without force. Gradualness and moderation protect against overload. Regularity builds the skill, and monitoring changes over time shows whether the practice matches the goal set.

If even one link is removed, the method is easily distorted: observation turns into anxious control, reducing breathing into endurance, and the control pause into a competition.

Common distortions of the basic principles

Distortions include trying to breathe as little as possible, holding back the inhalation by force, doing maximum breath holds, continuing an exercise despite worsening and judging the result by a single unusual sensation. Another mistake is ignoring factors that deepen breathing between sessions.

This material is for information and does not replace a doctor’s consultation and treatment. Do not cancel or change medicines and their doses on your own. Chest pain, pronounced difficulty breathing or loss of consciousness require emergency help. Breath holds must not be performed in water, while driving or where fainting is dangerous. Results are not guaranteed.
