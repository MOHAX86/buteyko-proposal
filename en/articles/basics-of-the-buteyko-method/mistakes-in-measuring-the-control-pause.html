Mistakes in measuring the control pause

Author: Alik Mullakhmetov, VEDB senior instructor and examiner, more than 30 years of practicing the Buteyko method

Updated: November 25, 2026

In short. Measuring the CP is hampered by a lack of rest, an altered inhalation or exhalation and unnecessary endurance, and comparing results is hampered by differing conditions. The main check is the first breath and the breathing after the pause.

A measurement error does not mean that your health has worsened. More often it shows that the initial conditions, the technique or the end criterion have changed.

Mistakes before the breath hold begins

Measuring without rest, deliberately changing your breathing, a new posture or body tension make the result incomparable.

Measuring without prior rest

Talking, walking or rushing immediately before the CP affect your current breathing. First you need to sit quietly for several minutes.

Deliberately reducing or deepening breathing

“Adjusting” your breathing beforehand changes the starting point and can artificially change the number.

Changing posture and body tension

Bending forward, raised shoulders, a drawn-in abdomen and tension during the hold break the uniformity of measurements.

Mistakes at the moment of exhalation

Too deep an inhalation before the pause or a forced exhalation create conditions that do not exist in the ordinary breathing cycle.

Too deep an inhalation before the pause

A reserve of air before the hold can artificially lengthen the result.

Forced or excessive exhalation

Trying to empty the lungs completely changes the sensations and does not match the definition of the CP.

Mistakes during the pause

Waiting for severe suffocation, continuing by willpower and aiming for a desired number turn the control pause into a different test.

Waiting for severe suffocation

The CP ends before severe discomfort.

Continuing by willpower

If you have to actively endure, the control part is already over.

Aiming for a desired number

Looking back at your previous record keeps you from noticing the real moment when the pause ends.

Mistakes after the pause

A deep recovery breath and faster breathing show that the hold was excessive. Trying to outwardly hide the recovery does not correct the result.

A deep recovery breath

The stronger the need to inhale, the more likely it is that the CP was drawn out too long.

Faster breathing and trying to hide it

What should be assessed is the natural reaction, not how well a person can suppress it.

Mistakes in recording and comparing

Rounding, choosing the best attempt and comparing different conditions create a false picture of changes over time.

Rounding and choosing the best attempt

Write down the first correct value exactly, not the largest one.

Comparing results from different conditions

The morning CP, a daytime pause and a measurement after an exercise serve different purposes.

How to correct your technique

Return to an ordinary exhalation and a single moderate end criterion: the first distinct signs of the need to inhale. After the pause, breathing should remain calm. The next scheduled measurement is more useful than immediate repeats for the sake of a better number.

When to ask an instructor to check your measurement

If the end point remains unclear, results differ sharply or you constantly need to recover after the CP, you should show your technique to an instructor.

This material is for information and does not replace a doctor’s consultation and treatment. Do not cancel or change medicines and their doses on your own. Chest pain, pronounced difficulty breathing or loss of consciousness require emergency help. Breath holds must not be performed in water, while driving or where fainting is dangerous. Results are not guaranteed.
