Deep breathing as understood in the Buteyko method

Author: Alik Mullakhmetov, VEDB senior instructor and examiner, more than 30 years of practicing the Buteyko method

Updated: November 25, 2026

In short. In the Buteyko method, “deep” refers not to a single big inhalation but to breathing that exceeds the body’s current needs. It can be quiet and almost imperceptible.

The phrase “deep breathing” seems clear until we try to define it precisely. In everyday speech it usually means a big, noticeable inhalation. In the Buteyko method the meaning is different: what is assessed is not the outward size of a single breath but whether total ventilation matches the body’s current needs.

Why the everyday and methodological meanings differ

The phrase “I took a deep breath” describes a single movement of the chest. It says nothing about breathing over the course of a minute or whether it matches a state of rest or exertion.

In the Buteyko method, what matters is a persistent disturbance of the breathing pattern. Under stress, the depth of inhalation may be one of the first parameters to change. The inhalation deepens and the minute volume of breathing increases. This is the origin of the expression “disease of deep breathing” in the school’s terminology.

Deep breathing as excessive ventilation

In the school’s physiological understanding, deep breathing is regarded as hyperventilation: ventilation of the lungs becomes excessive relative to the body’s current metabolic needs. The same volume of breathing therefore cannot be judged apart from the situation. What is natural during movement may be excessive at complete rest.

Volume of breathing

The depth of inhalation affects the amount of air passing through the airways in one cycle. If breath after breath becomes larger than necessary, the total minute volume of breathing rises.

Breathing rate

Excessive ventilation depends not only on depth but also on rate. Even breaths of moderate volume can create an excessive minute volume if breathing is too frequent. That is why the whole breathing pattern needs to be observed, not just one of its indicators.

Why excessive breathing can go unnoticed

Deep breathing is not necessarily noisy or sharp. A person may breathe quietly and almost imperceptibly, yet still more than is required at that moment. The outward impression helps observation but does not give a precise answer.

The excess may be sustained by habitual tension of the respiratory muscles, stress and other factors that deepen breathing. That is why the method directs attention not only to inhalations and exhalations but also to the conditions that deepen breathing again.

How the need for ventilation changes with exertion

During movement, breathing naturally increases. It is wrong to demand that a person breathe during any exertion the same way as when sitting at rest. The task of the method is not to make breathing minimal in all circumstances but to bring it into line with physiological need.

After exertion stops, breathing should gradually calm down. In methodological work, it is important to learn to notice how easily this happens and whether unnecessary breathing effort persists longer than needed.

Can deep breathing be identified by outward signs alone

Certain features can be noticed from the movement of the shoulders, chest and abdomen, the audibility of breathing and the breathing rate. But it is wrong to draw a final conclusion from a single outward sign. The school considers the state of breathing comprehensively, taking into account observation, the control pause, well-being and how symptoms change over time.

What mistakes arise when trying to reduce breathing

The most common mistake is to take the term literally and start suppressing the inhalation by force. A person tenses up, endures severe suffocation, makes abrupt breath holds and then has to recover their breath with deep breaths. This does not correspond to the principle of the method.

The reduction is achieved mainly through relaxation, down to a slight air shortage. Manageable work is acceptable, but not a struggle against the body. The goal is to remove excessive depth only down to the physiological norm, not to achieve the smallest possible breathing at any cost.

How this concept is used in the Buteyko method

Understanding deep breathing sets the direction of the whole practice. A person learns to notice excessive tidal volume, to relax, to work with factors that deepen breathing and to gradually normalize breathing.

Chest pain, pronounced difficulty breathing or loss of consciousness require emergency help. Breath holds must not be performed in water, while driving or where fainting is dangerous.
