K. P. Buteyko’s table of lung ventilation criteria: how to read it and what it shows

Author: Alik Mullakhmetov, VEDB senior instructor and examiner, more than 30 years of practicing the Buteyko method

Updated: November 25, 2026

In short. The table shows how, in the Buteyko model, lung ventilation, CO₂, pauses, breathing rate, pulse and a person’s condition are related. It should be read as a guide to direction, not as a diagnostic scale.

The table combines several indicators: the degree of the breathing disorder, carbon dioxide content, the control and maximum pauses, breathing and pulse rates, minute volume of breathing and the approximate depth of inhalation. Its main idea is to show the relationship between lung ventilation, the CO₂ level and the general condition of the body, not to evaluate a person by a single number.

This is a model of correspondences developed within the Buteyko method, not a modern diagnostic scale. It helps you see the direction of changes, but does not replace an examination, laboratory measurements and a doctor’s conclusion.

What this table is

According to Alik Mullakhmetov’s explanation, the table was compiled by Konstantin Pavlovich Buteyko in the 1960s–1970s on the basis of research carried out in a physiological laboratory, as well as clinical observations in medical institutions. The materials of this work are presented in the collection “Metod Buteyko. Opyt vnedreniya v meditsinskuyu praktiku”, published in 1990; K. P. Buteyko served as its compiling editor.

In the table, the states are arranged vertically: from marked deep breathing and a severe condition of the body, through the norm row, to the steps that the author attributed to increased endurance. Horizontally, the indicators are given that were meant to describe the same level of ventilation from different sides.

The main idea: the indicators are considered together

Lung ventilation depends not only on breathing rate. Minute volume of breathing is determined by how much air a person inhales in one cycle and how many such cycles they complete per minute. Therefore breathing can be excessive both with frequent breaths and with comparatively infrequent but deep ones.

CO₂ is produced in the tissues during metabolism, carried by the blood to the lungs and removed on exhalation. If alveolar ventilation is excessive relative to CO₂ production, its partial pressure falls. Modern physiology also regards PaCO₂ as an indicator closely linked to alveolar ventilation. For arterial blood, a PaCO₂ reference range of 35–45 mmHg is usually given; the values of a particular person are assessed with regard to the clinical situation and the method of measurement.

The Buteyko table tries to show a single chain:

  • the depth and minute volume of breathing change;
  • the removal of CO₂ through the lungs changes;
  • this is reflected in the tolerance of a breath hold;
  • breathing rate, pulse and well-being may change at the same time.

How to read the main columns

Condition of the body and degree of the breathing disorder

A step in the table is the level to which the author assigned a set of indicators. No single column describes the body completely. Even the same control pause in two people may be combined with different well-being, pulse rate, illnesses, medicine use and response to physical load.

CO₂ in percent and in millimeters of mercury

In the table, CO₂ is presented as a gas fraction and as partial pressure. These two forms are related through barometric pressure, but in an actual measurement it is necessary to specify which indicator is meant: CO₂ at the end of exhalation, in alveolar gas or in arterial blood. These are close but not identical values.

A capnograph measures CO₂ in exhaled air and usually shows its value at the end of exhalation, EtCO₂. Arterial blood gas analysis determines PaCO₂. Therefore a number found in a row of the table on the basis of the control pause should be called an approximate correspondence within the Buteyko model, not the result of a CO₂ measurement.

Control pause

The control pause is measured after a calm, ordinary exhalation, until the first distinct need to inhale, without willfully prolonging it and without a deep compensatory inhalation afterwards. In the method it is used primarily to observe changes in breathing over time.

The duration of a voluntary breath hold depends not only on CO₂. It is affected by the initial volume of air in the lungs, preceding breathing, the sensitivity of the respiratory center and chemoreceptors, the oxygen level, fitness, tension, expectation of a result and the technique of performance. Therefore it is impossible to calculate CO₂ content accurately or to establish a diagnosis from a single control pause.

Maximum and automatic pauses

The maximum pause is a longer breath hold with a willpower component. According to Alik Mullakhmetov, in the modern practice of the method it is almost never used for regular monitoring. In the original studies, the automatic pause was determined instrumentally as the natural interval after a calm exhalation; in the table it reflects a daily average characteristic of the breathing rhythm, not a hold that a person deliberately performs on command.

Breathing rate, minute volume and depth of inhalation

Breathing rate shows the number of breathing cycles per minute. Minute volume of breathing is the total volume of air passing through the lungs in a minute. Depth of inhalation, or tidal volume, is the volume of one breathing cycle. There is a calculated relationship between them: minute volume is approximately equal to breathing rate multiplied by tidal volume.

In the table, some of these values are presented as a calculated correspondence to a particular step. The image of the table must not be used as a standalone calculator: instrumental methods are used to determine ventilation accurately, and the formulas and calculation conditions must be taken from the primary methodology.

Pulse rate

Pulse is included as an additional indicator of general condition. It may change along with the load on the body, but it depends on many factors: age, physical fitness, temperature, emotional state, medicines and illnesses. Therefore a pulse that matches a particular row does not mean that all the other indicators automatically match.

What the norm row means

In the Buteyko table, the norm row corresponds to a control pause of 60 seconds and CO₂ of 6.5%, or about 46 mmHg. In the author’s system, this row was understood as a state without manifestations of deep breathing and the symptoms associated with it.

This norm should not be confused with the modern laboratory reference range. In clinical practice, an arterial blood PaCO₂ of 35–45 mmHg is usually indicated, and the same approximate range of 35–45 mmHg is often given for EtCO₂. These indicators refer to different methods of measurement, and the limits may depend on the laboratory, the person’s condition, altitude above sea level and other conditions.

Consequently, the norm row in the table is a methodological reference point of Buteyko, not a universal laboratory standard for all people.

How to understand “disease”, “ideal health” and “super-health”

The terms reflect the author’s own scale. The lower part of the table shows an increasing degree of deep breathing and severity of condition. The norm row marks the reference point chosen by the author. The upper part describes states of increased tolerance of physical load and restrictions, which Buteyko linked with a higher control pause.

The expressions “ideal health” and “super-health” are not modern medical diagnoses. They must not be understood as proof of invulnerability to infections or of the ability to safely do without sleep, water or food. It is more correct to regard them as historical terms of the author’s model.

How to interpret the “Getting rid of diseases” column

This column lists diseases and symptoms whose improvement the author and his followers observed at different stages of the normalization of breathing. According to Alik Mullakhmetov’s explanation, the distribution was based on clinical observations carried out in hospitals and hospital departments, the materials of which were included in the collection “Metod Buteyko. Opyt vnedreniya v meditsinskuyu praktiku”.

However, this column cannot be used as a promise of a result. It does not follow from it that a particular control pause confirms the presence of a disease, guarantees the disappearance of symptoms or allows treatment to be stopped. Diseases have different causes and courses, and an improvement in one indicator does not prove recovery from a specific diagnosis.

The correct wording is this: the column reflects the sequence of clinical improvements historically claimed by K. P. Buteyko, which must be assessed with regard to the original publications, the individual condition and the modern requirements of evidence-based medicine.

Why the indicators may change

The control pause, breathing rate and pulse are sensitive to measurement conditions. The result may be affected by:

  • time of day and sleep quality;
  • physical exercise and the time of recovery after it;
  • stress, anxiety and concentration on the result;
  • food, caffeine, alcohol and smoking;
  • air temperature, pain, infection and a flare-up of a disease;
  • medicines;
  • deepening your breathing beforehand and technique errors.

Therefore, to observe changes over time, the control pause is measured in comparable conditions. The most indicative is a regular morning measurement before food and exercise, after several minutes of quiet rest. The trend over weeks matters more than one random number.

What the table is useful for today

When used with caution, the table is useful. It shows that breathing cannot be assessed by its rate alone; it reminds us of the link between alveolar ventilation and CO₂; it combines a subjective test, observable indicators and instrumental values; it helps explain why the method tracks not a breath-hold record but a stable trend in calm breathing.

In practice, it is better to use the table as a map of hypotheses and directions of observation:

  • control pause: measure it using a single method and compare it with your own previous values;
  • breathing rate and pulse: treat them as additional indicators;
  • CO₂: if necessary, measure it with an appropriate device, without substituting a calculation based on the pause for the measurement;
  • symptoms and diseases: discuss them with your treating doctor, without drawing conclusions from one row of the table.

Main conclusion

What is distinctive about the table of lung ventilation criteria is its attempt to present breathing as an integral system of interrelated indicators. In it, the control pause, CO₂, minute ventilation, depth and rate of breathing, pulse and well-being are considered not in isolation, but as different manifestations of one process.

The main practical value of the table lies neither in literally predicting diseases nor in striving to get into a particular row at any cost. It helps you understand the direction of the work: to reduce excess ventilation, form calm breathing and track stable changes in comparable conditions.
