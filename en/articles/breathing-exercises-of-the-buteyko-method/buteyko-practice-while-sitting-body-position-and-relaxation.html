Buteyko practice while sitting: body position and relaxation

Author: Alik Mullakhmetov, VEDB senior instructor and examiner, more than 30 years of practicing the Buteyko method

Updated: November 25, 2026

In short. Sit in a stable position: feet on the floor, back naturally straight, shoulders and arms relaxed, abdomen and chest free. The posture should be neither slack nor tensely “perfect.”

A seated position is often used in Buteyko practice: it lets you stay stable and observe your breathing. The purpose of the posture is to remove unnecessary muscular work and create conditions for relaxation.

Why pay attention to body position

An uncomfortable posture is distracting and forces the body to keep its balance. An overly rigid posture, on the other hand, increases tension and may deepen inhalation.

The body should be supported by the chair and the feet. If you have to keep adjusting your position, it will be hard to relax.

Clothing, a belt, and the position of your torso should not press on your abdomen and chest.

How to sit for practice

Sit closer to the middle of the chair or use the backrest as your instructor has recommended. Choose the option that keeps you stable.

Foot support and pelvis position

Your feet rest fully on the floor and your knees are in a stable position. Your pelvis is not tilted sharply backward or to the side.

Back, head, and neck

Your back is naturally upright. Do not arch it, and do not strain to stretch up through the crown of your head. Your head continues the line of the spine.

Hands on your knees

Your hands rest quietly on your knees or thighs. This reduces the work of the shoulder girdle.

What to relax while sitting

Check your face, jaw, shoulders, hands, chest, and abdomen.

Relax your facial expression, let your shoulders drop, and do not clench your fingers.

Do not push your chest out or pull your abdomen in. The breathing movements remain free.

Common posture mistakes

Slouching and excessive straightening require the muscles to compensate. Look for a middle, stable position.

A pulled-in abdomen and raised shoulders hinder relaxation and may give the impression of reduced breathing when it is really caused by clamping.

Crossed or unsupported legs, perching on the edge of the chair, and constant movement make observation difficult.

How to check that a posture suits you

A suitable posture can be held for several minutes without pain, struggle, or constant adjustments.

Stillness should not be forced. In case of numbness or pain, change your position.

If a posture makes you hold back your abdomen or chest, it is not suitable.

When to change position or take a break

Take a break in case of pain, dizziness, growing discomfort, or inability to relax. Individual limitations are discussed with your instructor and, if necessary, with a doctor.

Conclusion

A good seated posture serves relaxation and observation, not a display of perfect bearing.
If you spend a lot of time at a desk, also read about how to help your neck, shoulders, and back during sedentary work.

This material is for information and does not replace a doctor’s consultation and treatment. Do not cancel or change medicines and their doses on your own. Chest pain, pronounced difficulty breathing or loss of consciousness require emergency help. Breath holds must not be performed in water, while driving or where fainting is dangerous. Results are not guaranteed.
