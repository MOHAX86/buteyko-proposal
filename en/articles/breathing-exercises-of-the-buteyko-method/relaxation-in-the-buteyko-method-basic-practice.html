Relaxation in the Buteyko method: basic practice

Author: Alik Mullakhmetov, VEDB senior instructor and examiner, more than 30 years of practicing the Buteyko method

Updated: November 25, 2026

In short. In the Buteyko method, relaxation is the main way to reduce excessive depth of inhalation without forcing the breath. The goal is not to switch off breathing but to help it approach the physiological norm.

A person learns to free the face, shoulders, chest, abdomen, and respiratory muscles from unnecessary effort and observes how the breathing pattern changes.

Why relaxation affects breathing

Inhalation is produced by muscle work. With emotional or physical tension, additional effort may join in the breathing, and the inhalation deepens. Reducing unnecessary tension helps decrease ventilation.

Raised shoulders, a clenched jaw, a tense chest, and a pulled-in abdomen often accompany a more active inhalation. A person may not notice them until deliberately directing attention to the body.

Relaxation as a way to reduce ventilation

When unnecessary effort decreases, inhalation can become calmer without a rigid command to “breathe less.” This approach corresponds to the school’s methodological position. When a slight air shortage appears, the person relaxes within it, without struggling.

What exactly a person learns to relax

Relaxation begins with areas where tension is easier to notice and gradually extends to the respiratory muscles.

Face, shoulders, and chest

Free your forehead, eyelids, and jaw, lower your shoulders, and stop deliberately lifting your chest as you inhale. There is no need to hold your torso fixed on purpose.

Abdomen and respiratory muscles

The abdomen is not pulled in or held hard. The task is to reduce unnecessary activity while keeping the natural breathing movements.

How to observe relaxation

Observation should be calm. There is no need to judge each breath as right or wrong.

Do not control every breathing movement

Excessive control itself becomes a source of tension. Attention notes the changes but does not command every muscle.

Notice the decrease in inhalation effort

The guide is the feeling that less muscular work is required to inhale. Breathing becomes quieter and calmer.

What result is considered correct

Correct relaxation does not leave behind severe suffocation or exhaustion.

A calmer breathing pattern

After practice, breathing should recover without big breaths and noticeable struggle.

Improvement in CP, symptoms, and condition

The response is assessed by the control pause, familiar symptoms, and well-being. All three indicators are considered together.

Why relaxation is not the same as passivity

Relaxing is an active skill. Willpower shows itself in organizing the session, holding your attention, reducing the load in time, and working with everyday factors.

The role of regularity and the instructor

The skill is formed through repetition. The instructor helps you notice hidden tension and avoid replacing relaxation with muscular suppression of inhalation.

This material is for information and does not replace a doctor’s consultation and treatment. Do not cancel or change medicines and their doses on your own. Chest pain, pronounced difficulty breathing or loss of consciousness require emergency help. Breath holds must not be performed in water, while driving or where fainting is dangerous. Results are not guaranteed.
