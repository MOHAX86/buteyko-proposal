Reducing the depth of breathing: a basic exercise

Author: Alik Mullakhmetov, VEDB senior instructor and examiner, more than 30 years of practicing the Buteyko method

Updated: November 25, 2026

In short. Sitting quietly, you gradually reduce the depth of inhalation through relaxation, not force, to the point of a slight air shortage. The goal is the physiological norm, not the smallest possible breathing.

The exercise is performed mainly through relaxation. A person does not block inhalation by force but reduces unnecessary muscular effort while maintaining a relatively comfortable air shortage.

The goal of the basic exercise

The goal is to make breathing calmer and remove the part of ventilation that exceeds the body’s current need. Minimal breathing in itself is not the correct result.

With normalization, sufficient natural breathing is preserved. The person does not try to stop the work of the respiratory system.

Excessive reduction creates strong suffocation and tension. This is a sign of a mistake, not of more effective performance.

Preparing for the exercise

Before starting, you need a few minutes of rest. The practice is done in a safe place, without rushing, and not right after intense exertion.

It is usually comfortable to sit with your feet supported, your back straight, and your hands on your knees. The position should let you relax rather than make you hold yourself up with effort.

Observing your usual breathing

At first, change nothing. Notice how active the inhalation is, whether your shoulders rise, and whether your face, chest, and abdomen are tense.

How the depth of inhalation is reduced

The reduction begins not with forbidding yourself to inhale but with lowering muscular effort. As you relax, the inhalation becomes quieter and smaller.

Do not pull in your abdomen, clamp your chest, or block your throat. Such actions create an outward reduction of inhalation but increase internal tension.

Lower the effort little by little. Breathing should remain continuous and under control, without convulsive breaths.

Which sensations are acceptable

A moderate, relatively comfortable need to inhale a little more is possible. With the correct load, a person is able to stay relaxed.

Such an air shortage is felt, but it does not take over all your attention or force you to struggle with your breathing.

Signs of an excessive load

Strong suffocation, panic, marked muscle tension, dizziness, worsening symptoms, and a need to breathe deeply after the exercise require stopping the load.

How to finish the exercise

Do not break off the practice with a sharp deep breath. Ease off the reduction, let your breathing calmly return to its usual pattern, and assess your condition.

If a series of big breaths is needed after the session, the load was too high or the finish too abrupt.

Assessing CP, symptoms, and well-being

After recovery, the control pause, familiar symptoms, and general condition are compared with the readings before the exercise. Deterioration by these criteria indicates a need for correction.

Why an instructor checks the exercise

It is difficult to distinguish relaxation from hidden restraint of inhalation on your own. The instructor sees your posture, muscle reactions, and recovery, and then corrects the load.

Brief reminder

Calm down → sit comfortably → observe → relax the inhalation muscles → allow a moderate air shortage → do not endure severe suffocation → finish gently → assess the response. The practice is learned with an instructor.

This material is for information and does not replace a doctor’s consultation and treatment. Do not cancel or change medicines and their doses on your own. Chest pain, pronounced difficulty breathing or loss of consciousness require emergency help. Breath holds must not be performed in water, while driving or where fainting is dangerous. Results are not guaranteed.
