Breathing trainers: 11 remarks on the article by Agadzhanyan and Deineka

Author: Alik Mullakhmetov, VEDB senior instructor and examiner, more than 30 years of practicing the Buteyko method

Updated: November 25, 2026

Author | Alik Mullakhmetov

In short. In the assessment of the author of this review, the article by Agadzhanyan and Deineka mixes up the name of a physiological effect, and some of the conclusions about CO₂ and rebreathing are insufficiently substantiated. This does not yet prove either that the breathing trainer is dangerous or that it is ineffective.

Academic authority does not exempt a scientific publication from scrutiny. This is especially important when physiological explanations are used to justify methods of acting on breathing. Below I examine a specific article by N. A. Agadzhanyan and E. A. Deineka on respiratory homeostasis and show which of its propositions I consider erroneous and which insufficiently substantiated.

This is the publication “Проблемы восстановления дыхательного гомеостаза при гипер- и гипокапнических состояниях: реабилитационные стратегии и их физиологическое обоснование” (Problems of restoring respiratory homeostasis in hyper- and hypocapnic states: rehabilitation strategies and their physiological rationale), published in “Вестник восстановительной медицины” (Bulletin of Restorative Medicine) in 2009, No. 2 (30), pages 4–8 [1].

The subject of this review is the arguments of that work, above all the rationale for rebreathing with the TFI capnicator breathing trainer. Assessing one article is not the same as assessing the entire scientific legacy of its authors. But neither does the authors’ scientific standing make a disputed explanation proven.

What exactly needs to be checked

Rebreathing involves inhaling part of the exhaled air again. In the article under analysis, this principle is considered as a way to change the CO₂ content in the body in combination with hypoxic exposure.

To assess such a concept, three questions need to be distinguished: whether the physiological mechanism is described correctly, whether a beneficial result is confirmed and whether the safety of a specific regimen of use has been studied. An answer to one of them does not replace answers to the others.

There is also an important caveat: in their conclusion, Agadzhanyan and Deineka themselves note the limitations of the approaches considered and the need for further research. My remarks concern the accuracy of the argumentation and the sufficiency of the data presented, not a final solution to all the questions supposedly claimed by the authors.

1. The magnitude of partial pressure does not prove tolerance of a gas

On page 6, the authors support the assumption of a higher tissue tolerance to CO₂ by comparing the tissue tensions of carbon dioxide and oxygen.

Such a comparison does not in itself show how resistant cells are to an excess or deficiency of each gas. Partial pressure characterizes the state of a gas, whereas tolerance characterizes permissible changes and their consequences. Different characteristics are being compared.

To confirm greater tolerance, data are needed on the ranges of exposure, duration and functional impairments. The absolute values given in the article do not replace such data. I therefore consider this argument methodologically insufficient.

2. A deviation in CO₂ cannot automatically be equated with ischemia

On page 6, long-persisting hypercapnia and hypocapnia are linked with the subsequent development of ischemia. The text gives PaCO₂ values above 46 and below 34 mm Hg.

PaCO₂ is the partial pressure of carbon dioxide in arterial blood. Ischemia means an insufficient blood supply to tissue. Merely crossing a numerical PaCO₂ threshold proves neither the presence of ischemia nor the inevitability of its occurrence.

The difference between vascular reactions is also important: a decrease and an increase in CO₂ cannot be combined into one causal chain without considering the specific condition. It is necessary to assess the severity of the disturbance, its duration, the blood circulation and the oxygen supply of the tissues. A statement about an obligatory final outcome requires more rigorous substantiation.

3. Hypoxemia and tissue hypoxia require consistent differentiation

Hypoxemia is a decrease in oxygen in arterial blood. Tissue hypoxia is an oxygen supply to tissues that is insufficient relative to their needs. These concepts are related but not identical.

The section on page 6 uses the category of hypocapnic hypoxemia, after which mechanisms of tissue hypoxia and impaired blood supply are discussed. In my view, the transition between these levels of description is not clear enough.

In primary hyperventilation with preserved pulmonary gas exchange, a decrease in CO₂ can be combined with normal or elevated PaO₂. It is therefore important to consider separately hypocapnia with preserved arterial oxygenation and conditions in which oxygen in the blood decreases at the same time. Otherwise the reader gets the false impression that one necessarily follows from the other.

4. The Verigo–Bohr effect is called the Haldane effect

Page 4 gives the explanation: “The Haldane effect: when there is a lack of CO₂ in the blood, tissue oxygenation is impeded.”

Here the directions of the mutual influence of the respiratory gases are mixed up. The Verigo–Bohr effect describes the influence of CO₂ and the acidity of the medium on the affinity of hemoglobin for oxygen. The Haldane effect is the influence of hemoglobin oxygenation on the transport of CO₂ and hydrogen ions. These relationships are studied and distinguished in works on hemoglobin physiology [2].

The phrase in question concerns the influence of a lack of CO₂ on oxygen supply, so it should have been linked with the Verigo–Bohr effect. This is a specific terminological error, not a matter of one’s attitude toward one breathing school or another.

5. The effect of CO₂ disturbances cannot be reduced to hypoxia alone

In the summary statement on page 6, the damaging effect of hypo- and hypercapnia is in effect reduced to hypoxia. Yet on the preceding pages the authors themselves mention acid-base, nervous and other effects of CO₂.

The problem, therefore, is not a complete absence of this information but an inconsistency in the generalization. Changes in CO₂, the acid-base state and oxygen supply interact, but this does not make hypoxia the only possible mechanism of functional impairment.

An experiment by Briva and co-authors is telling: in experiments on rats and cell models, elevated CO₂ impaired the function of the alveolar epithelium independently of pH [3]. This is not a study of a breathing trainer and not proof of its harm. However, it shows why the universal reduction of all CO₂ effects to oxygen deficiency needs to be reconsidered.

6. Compensating for consequences and eliminating the cause are different tasks

In the conclusion on page 7, the authors acknowledge: “The rebreathing method does not eliminate the psycho-emotional or hypercompensatory preconditions themselves for the formation of hyperventilation syndrome.”

This is an important limitation. If the composition of the inhaled mixture changes but the circumstances that sustain excessive ventilation remain, a change in readings during the procedure does not yet prove a lasting restoration of independent breathing after it ends.

Acting on a consequence is not in itself a scientific error: compensating methods can have practical value. But they cannot on that basis be declared to eliminate the cause. Such a conclusion requires separate observations after the device exposure has stopped. It is precisely this transition from temporary compensation to stable independent regulation that I do not find proven in the work under review.

7. The term “hypoxic load” needs measurable criteria

The authors describe the operation of the TFI as rebreathing combined with a moderate hypoxic load. However, the word “moderate” by itself does not say what exactly changes and by how much.

A decrease in oxygen in the inhaled mixture, a change in its pressure in the alveoli, arterial hypoxemia and tissue hypoxia are different levels of assessment. They need to be distinguished.

A reproducible description requires the composition of the mixture, the conditions of the procedure and the recorded changes in readings. In the description of the TFI under discussion, these data are insufficient to independently assess the claimed hypoxic component. Consequently, the term denotes a presumed effect but does not yet provide its quantitative substantiation.

8. The benefit of the hypoxic component is not separated from other effects

Rebreathing can simultaneously change the conditions of O₂ and CO₂ intake. Even if an improvement is observed after the procedure, the observation itself does not yet establish which of the effects produced the result.

A separate question arises: why should an additional decrease in oxygen help a person who is already presumed to have impaired tissue oxygen supply? The answer requires an assessment of the initial condition and a controlled comparison of the effects.

Without such separation, a possible beneficial effect cannot be attributed specifically to the hypoxic component. This remark concerns the evidence for the mechanism, not a claim that any dosed hypoxic exposure is necessarily harmful.

9. Rebreathing and normalizing one’s own ventilation are not the same thing

The rebreathed CO₂ may originally have been formed in the body. But after exhalation it returns from an external gas mixture. This path differs from a change in its elimination when one’s own alveolar ventilation is normalized.

A CO₂ molecule does not acquire different chemical properties because of its origin. What differs is the conditions of exposure and the response of the respiratory system. Therefore, an increase in a single gas reading should not automatically be called a restoration of the whole regulatory system.

There is also the question of how the mixture is delivered. When a mouthpiece is used, the air bypasses the nasal cavity; this changes the conditions of breathing. This remark applies specifically to the version with a mouthpiece: the article also mentions a mask. It would be wrong to extend it without qualification to all possible designs.

10. Dosage and individual response cannot be replaced by a general thesis about the benefit of CO₂

Inhaling additional CO₂ can change ventilation, arterial CO₂ and pH. Ellingsen and co-authors measured these values in five healthy people inhaling mixtures with 1% and 2% CO₂. Despite increased ventilation, arterial CO₂ rose and pH fell [4].

This experiment shows that the exposure is physiologically active, but it does not determine the effectiveness or safety of the TFI. Assessing a specific device requires its own data: composition of the mixture, duration, conditions of use, initial condition and individual reaction.

The principle of comparison is also important: the CO₂ concentration in a room, in the inhaled mixture, in the alveoli and in the blood is not one and the same value. Room ventilation standards cannot be directly turned into a therapeutic dosage or proof of the toxicity of a brief procedure. The description of the TFI does not contain enough such grounds for assessing the regimen.

11. Assessment of the returned mixture should not be limited to O₂ and CO₂

Exhaled breath contains not only the main respiratory gases but also volatile compounds. Phillips and co-authors found about 2000 different volatile organic compounds in breath samples from 34 healthy volunteers. Not all of these compounds necessarily originate inside the body [5].

In a study by O’Hara and co-authors, rebreathing in a special analytical circuit changed the measured concentrations of isoprene, acetone and methanol [6]. The study was devoted to collecting breath samples, not to toxicological testing of the TFI. Its numerical results cannot be transferred to another device.

Nevertheless, it helps formulate a question for assessing the mixture: which substances actually come back, in what concentrations, and how do these concentrations change? Detecting a trace component does not yet mean danger. A conclusion requires measurements and comparison with toxicologically significant levels. The article under review presents no such assessment of the returned mixture.

What studies show and what they do not establish

Works on hemoglobin make it possible to distinguish the Bohr and Haldane effects. Experiments with CO₂ show that adding it to the inhaled mixture causes a measurable response. Breath studies confirm the complexity of its composition and the influence of the sampling method on the concentrations of individual components.

But these works are not direct trials of the effectiveness and safety of the TFI breathing trainer. Nor can studies published after 2009 be used to reproach the authors for not knowing future results. They are needed for a modern assessment of the questions that remained open.

One should therefore distinguish between an established terminological error, a disputable causal model and the absence of specific measurements in one publication. These are grounds for criticism of differing strength.

Why this analysis matters for understanding the Buteyko method

In the approach of Alik Mullakhmetov’s breathing school, work with the Buteyko method is aimed at normalizing a person’s own breathing mainly through relaxation and work with factors that deepen breathing. Changing the composition of the inhaled mixture with a device is a different kind of intervention.

The difference between the approaches needs to be understood, and their results need to be assessed against the relevant data. Criticism of the rationale for a breathing trainer does not in itself serve as comparative evidence that another technique is superior. What matters more for the reader is learning to ask precise questions: what mechanism is proposed, what was measured, and what conclusion actually follows from the results.

Result

The article by Agadzhanyan and Deyneka contains a specific confusion in the name of a physiological effect, debatable generalizations, and questions that cannot be answered for lack of quantitative data. As presented, its argumentation is insufficient to confirm all the mechanisms of device-based breathing correction under discussion.

At the same time, the authors themselves acknowledge limitations and propose further research. The shortcomings identified do not automatically prove that a particular device is either dangerous or ineffective. They show which statements require clarification and independent verification.

Scientific authority is important as part of a professional biography. Proof is determined by the precision of concepts, the quality of data, and the soundness of conclusions.
