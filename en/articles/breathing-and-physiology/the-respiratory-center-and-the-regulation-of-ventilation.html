The respiratory center and the regulation of ventilation

Author: Alik Mullakhmetov, VEDB senior instructor and examiner, more than 30 years of practicing the Buteyko method

Updated: November 25, 2026

In short. Breathing is controlled not by a single point but by a network of neurons in the brain. It takes into account blood CO2, pH, and oxygen, as well as signals from movement and emotions.

Not a single point but a network

The expression “respiratory center” is convenient, but it may create the wrong impression of one small point that switches on inhalation. In fact, the rhythm and pattern of breathing are shaped by a network of neurons in the medulla oblongata and the pons that interacts with the cortex, the limbic system, the spinal cord, and peripheral receptors.

This network controls the breathing muscles automatically, but it constantly changes the command in accordance with metabolism, movement, speech, sleep, and emotional state.

How the breathing rhythm is formed

Groups of neurons in the brainstem create and reshape rhythmic activity. The signal travels along motor nerves to the diaphragm and other breathing muscles. Inhalation gives way to exhalation, and the rate and force of contractions adjust to the body’s needs.

The rhythm is not a rigid metronome. It changes during swallowing, coughing, laughing, talking, and movement in order to coordinate breathing with other actions.

Central chemoreceptors

The central chemosensitive structures respond primarily to a change in the pH of the fluid around the brain, which is linked to the passage of CO2 across the blood-brain barrier. When arterial CO2 rises, pH falls and the ventilatory stimulus increases.

With a prolonged change in CO2, the composition of the fluid and the sensitivity of the system partially readjust. Therefore, the response in acute and chronic states may differ.

Peripheral chemoreceptors

The carotid bodies at the bifurcation of the carotid arteries and the aortic bodies receive information about the composition of arterial blood. They respond to a fall in PaO2, as well as to changes in CO2 and pH.

The response to oxygen becomes especially strong when the arterial partial pressure of O2 falls noticeably. At an ordinary oxygen level, the system sensitive to CO2 and pH remains the main constant chemical regulator of ventilation.

Other signals

During physical exertion, ventilation increases even before blood gases change significantly. Commands from the motor areas of the brain and signals from the muscles and joints take part in this. Temperature, pain, fever, and hormonal influences can also change breathing.

Emotions act through the cortex and the limbic system. That is why fear can deepen breathing even without any increase in muscular work. Voluntary control makes it possible to change the rhythm for a short time, but automatic regulation continues to protect the gas composition.

What regulation does not measure directly

There is no single receptor that continuously measures “tissue hypoxia of the whole body”. Local tissues respond to a lack of oxygen with their own metabolic and vascular mechanisms, while the systemic ventilatory response depends especially on arterial gases, pH, and nerve signals.

Regulation of breathing and the Buteyko method

Conscious practice takes place against the background of automatic regulation. Its task is to remove excess effort and the habitual factors that deepen breathing, not to defeat the respiratory center. That is why breathing should be reduced gently, through relaxation, without moving into strenuous endurance.
