Breathing during sleep: how it differs from wakefulness

Author: Alik Mullakhmetov, VEDB senior instructor and examiner, more than 30 years of practicing the Buteyko method

Updated: November 25, 2026

In short. During sleep there is no conscious control; breathing is regulated automatically and changes with the sleep stages. The habit of breathing excessively deeply may persist at night too.

While awake, a person can notice how they are breathing, relax, and consciously reduce excessive depth of breathing. During sleep there is no such control, and the habitual breathing pattern may persist or become stronger.

What happens as you fall asleep

During the day, breathing depends not only on the body’s need for oxygen and carbon dioxide. It is influenced by speech, movement, emotions, attention, and conscious control. As you fall asleep, these influences weaken, and the regulation of breathing becomes predominantly automatic.

Normally, during the transition to sleep, ventilation usually decreases slightly and the level of carbon dioxide in arterial blood rises a little. On the border of sleep, the breathing rhythm may temporarily become less stable. Small fluctuations in the depth and rate of breathing do not in themselves indicate a disorder.

However, this general tendency does not mean that every person’s breathing becomes calm and optimal during sleep.

Breathing in slow-wave sleep

In stable slow-wave sleep, or NREM sleep, breathing usually becomes more regular. At the same time, the response of the respiratory center to a rise in carbon dioxide and a fall in oxygen weakens. This means that with the same changes in blood gas composition, the body’s response during sleep may be weaker than during wakefulness.

In addition, the tone of the upper airway muscles decreases. In most healthy people the airways remain open, but with an anatomical predisposition, nasal congestion, and some other conditions, airway resistance may increase.

Breathing in REM sleep

During rapid eye movement, or REM, sleep, the activity of the brain and the autonomic nervous system becomes more variable. Breathing may be less regular: its rate and depth change, and short pauses sometimes occur.

In this stage, the skeletal muscles relax considerably. The diaphragm continues to work, but the involvement of some accessory breathing muscles decreases. In a healthy person this usually causes no problems, but it may matter in diseases of the respiratory or neuromuscular system.

Why habitual deep breathing may increase during sleep

A pattern of deep, noisy, or irregular breathing that has become established during the day may persist at night and become more pronounced.

The following may contribute to this:

  • lying down;
  • prolonged sleep, especially on the back;
  • muscle relaxation;
  • opening the mouth;
  • difficulty with nasal breathing;
  • increased resistance in the upper airways;
  • an already formed habit of breathing excessively deeply.

If ventilation turns out to be higher than the body requires at rest, carbon dioxide is removed at an increased rate. Within the physiological concept of the Buteyko method, this is regarded as one of the reasons why a person may wake up feeling drained, with a dry mouth, a blocked nose, or a headache, and why their morning control pause may be lower than the evening one.

At the same time, a single control pause cannot be used to diagnose a disease or determine the cause of feeling unwell. The reading depends on the measurement conditions, sleep, stress, the condition of the airways, and other factors.

Upper airways, snoring, and pauses in breathing

During sleep, the tongue, soft palate, and walls of the pharynx are supported less actively by the muscles. If the airway is narrow to begin with, they may partially or completely collapse. As a result, snoring, episodes of restricted airflow, or obstructive pauses in breathing appear.

The likelihood of such disorders depends on anatomical features, body weight, age, sleeping position, alcohol use, taking sedative medicines, and other factors. An open mouth may accompany disordered breathing, but on its own it does not make it possible to establish the cause.

Loud regular snoring, pauses in breathing noticed by family members, waking up choking, morning headaches, marked daytime sleepiness, and poorly controlled high blood pressure require seeing a doctor and having a sleep study.

Breathing practice does not replace the diagnosis and treatment of apnea. Taping the mouth also cannot be considered a way of treating obstruction: with difficult nasal breathing and some other conditions, it may be unsafe.

Breathing during sleep and the Buteyko method

In the Buteyko method, work with breathing is carried out not only in the morning but also before sleep. A methodological manual on Voluntary Elimination of Deep Breathing explains that evening training is needed so that a person falls asleep with calm, minimal breathing and it increases less toward morning. Morning practice helps to reduce deep breathing after sleep.

The goal is not to control every breath during the night, which is impossible. The breathing pattern needs to be gradually normalized during wakefulness, so that calm, quiet nasal breathing becomes habitual and, as far as possible, is maintained during sleep.

To observe changes, you can compare the evening and morning control pauses, assessing the readings under the same conditions. At the same time, it is important to pay attention to how you feel after waking, the quality of sleep, and whether there is snoring, dry mouth, or waking at night.

If the morning control pause is regularly lower than the evening one, this may indicate that breathing increases at night, but it is not a diagnosis in itself. The condition of the nasal and upper airways, body position during sleep, and possible sleep disorders need to be taken into account.

Brief conclusion

During sleep, the upper airway muscles relax, and the breathing rhythm depends on the sleep stage. In a healthy person, ventilation usually decreases slightly, but excessively deep breathing that has become habitual may persist.

That is why regular daytime and evening practice is important in the Buteyko method: its task is to make calm, quiet nasal breathing a habit. At the same time, snoring, choking, and pauses in breathing require a separate examination, since breathing practice does not replace the diagnosis of sleep apnea.
