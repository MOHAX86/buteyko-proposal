Breathing and the work of the diaphragm

Author: Alik Mullakhmetov, VEDB senior instructor and examiner, more than 30 years of practicing the Buteyko method

Updated: November 25, 2026

In short. The diaphragm is the main muscle of quiet inhalation. In Buteyko practice, what matters is not a large range of its movement but reducing unnecessary breathing effort through relaxation.

Where the diaphragm is located

The diaphragm is a broad dome-shaped muscle separating the chest cavity from the abdominal cavity. Its muscle fibers attach to the lower ribs, the breastbone, and structures of the lower back and converge on a central tendon.

The esophagus, the aorta, the inferior vena cava, and other structures pass through the diaphragm. Therefore, its work is connected not only with the movement of air but also with changes in pressure in the chest and abdominal cavities.

How the diaphragm creates an inhalation

During quiet inhalation, the diaphragm contracts and moves down. The volume of the chest increases, intrapleural pressure becomes more negative, the lungs expand, and air flows in.

During quiet exhalation, the diaphragm relaxes. The elastic properties of the lungs and chest return the system to its initial volume. That is why an ordinary exhalation at rest is predominantly passive. In forced exhalation, the muscles of the abdominal wall and the internal intercostal muscles come into play.

The diaphragm does not work alone

The movement of the diaphragm is coordinated with the intercostal muscles, the lower ribs, and the abdominal wall. During inhalation, the lower part of the chest usually widens in several directions, and the abdominal organs shift slightly.

Body position changes the mechanical conditions. Slouching, a rigidly fixed abdomen, pain, or excessive tension can limit movement. But the condition of the diaphragm cannot be determined from a single sensation or the appearance of the abdomen.

What a person may feel

During quiet breathing, movement may be felt in the lower ribs, the sides of the torso, and the abdomen. These sensations differ from person to person. They depend on posture, body build, habitual tidal volume, and attention to the body.

A pronounced bulging of the abdomen is not a necessary sign of correct breathing. If a person deliberately takes the deepest possible “belly” breath, minute ventilation may increase. Diaphragmatic movement and a normal volume of ventilation are related but not identical concepts.

What a “clamped diaphragm” means

In everyday speech, this may describe tension under the ribs, inability to inhale freely, soreness, or a feeling of tightness. These complaints are real, but the term itself is not an exact diagnosis.

Similar sensations may arise from muscle overstrain, anxiety, digestive disorders, diseases of the heart and lungs, or problems of the musculoskeletal system. With persistent pain, shortness of breath, weakness, or a worsening condition, medical assessment is needed.

The diaphragm in the Buteyko method

In Buteyko practice, what matters is not a demonstratively large range of the diaphragm but reducing excessive breathing effort. Relaxing the face, shoulders, chest, and abdomen helps to remove unnecessary muscle involvement and bring ventilation closer to physiological demand.

You should not forcibly pull in or push out the abdomen, fix the chest, or endure a severe air shortage. The technique is adjusted according to the person’s condition and response to the exercise.

Brief conclusion

Effective work of the diaphragm does not require the deepest possible breathing and is not judged by the movement of the abdomen alone.
