Why ventilation changes during physical exercise

Author: Alik Mullakhmetov, VEDB senior instructor and examiner, more than 30 years of practicing the Buteyko method

Updated: November 25, 2026

In short. Working muscles consume more oxygen and produce more CO2, so breathing increases. This is normal as long as it matches the intensity of the work and the body’s capabilities.

What changes in working muscles

During movement, muscles need more energy. Oxygen consumption and CO2 production increase. Circulation intensifies to deliver oxygen and remove metabolic products. The respiratory system has to work together with the heart and blood vessels.

That is why breathing during exertion becomes deeper and then also faster. This in itself is not a disorder but a normal adaptation to increased CO2 production and oxygen demand.

Why ventilation increases

The rise in ventilation helps to keep arterial gases within a suitable range. During moderate exertion, PaO2 and PaCO2 in a healthy person often change little: increased ventilation matches increased metabolism.

This is an important distinction. A person may breathe noticeably harder but not hyperventilate in the physiological sense if CO2 production has risen proportionally.

How the response is triggered

At the start of movement, ventilation increases very quickly, before blood gases have had time to change significantly. The central command from the motor areas of the brain and signals from the working muscles and joints take part in this.

Then chemical feedback comes into play. Chemoreceptors respond to CO2, pH, and O2, and the body fine-tunes ventilation in line with the intensity of the work.

From moderate to high intensity

During light and moderate work, ventilation usually rises roughly in step with oxygen consumption and CO2 production. At higher intensity, the formation of lactate and hydrogen ions in the muscles increases. Bicarbonate takes part in buffering them, additional CO2 is formed, and ventilation increases disproportionately.

At even higher intensity, the rise in ventilation becomes especially pronounced. In sports physiology, these transitions are described as ventilatory thresholds. They depend on fitness, the type of exercise, and the person’s condition.

Nasal breathing as a practical guide

Being able to maintain calm nasal breathing can be a convenient guide to low or moderate exertion. If you have to open your mouth wide and your breathing is no longer controllable, the intensity has probably become high for your current condition.

But this is not an absolute medical test. Nasal congestion, temperature, altitude, the features of the training, and individual adaptation change this criterion. In some sports, mouth breathing at high power output is physiological.

Exertion and the Buteyko method

Physical activity increases CO2 production and helps to train the coordination of breathing with movement. In the Buteyko method, the load is chosen so that it does not turn into prolonged uncontrolled over-deep breathing.

After the work is finished, breathing should gradually recover. If chest pain, unusual shortness of breath, a feeling of near fainting, or a sharp worsening of how you feel occurs, stop the activity and seek medical assessment.
