Breathing and blood vessel tone

Author: Alik Mullakhmetov, VEDB senior instructor and examiner, more than 30 years of practicing the Buteyko method

Updated: November 25, 2026

In short. Breathing affects vascular tone through CO2, pH, nervous regulation, and changes in pressure. The link between CO2 and cerebral blood flow is especially noticeable, but different vessels respond differently.

What is vascular tone

The walls of arteries and arterioles contain smooth muscle cells. By changing their degree of contraction, the vessels regulate resistance to blood flow and the distribution of blood between organs. Vascular tone is influenced by the nervous system, hormones, pressure inside the vessel, and local metabolic products.

CO2 and the associated change in pH are important local factors. However, different vascular beds respond differently. That is why the statement “CO2 dilates all blood vessels” is too crude.

CO2 and the blood vessels of the brain

Cerebral blood flow is especially sensitive to arterial CO2. When it rises, the vessels of the brain usually dilate and blood flow increases; when CO2 falls, the vessels constrict and blood flow decreases. The change in pH around the vessel wall plays a significant role.

That is why rapid hyperventilation may be accompanied by dizziness, darkening of vision, a sense of unreality, and impaired concentration. These sensations do not prove a specific diagnosis, but they are consistent with the known response of cerebral circulation to hypocapnia.

Systemic vessels and working tissues

In the systemic circulation, the local accumulation of CO2, hydrogen ions, adenosine, and other metabolites helps to increase blood flow to active tissue. For example, a working muscle receives more blood in line with its needs.

But the overall response of the body is determined by more than one metabolite. The sympathetic nervous system may constrict some vessels, local factors may dilate others, and the heart changes its output. The result depends on the organ and the situation.

Why the vessels of the lungs behave differently

In the lungs, the key local signal is the oxygen content of the alveoli. If an area of the lung is poorly ventilated and alveolar O2 falls, the small pulmonary vessels in that area constrict. In this way, blood is redistributed to better ventilated alveoli.

This is the opposite of the typical response of systemic vessels to a lack of oxygen. Therefore, rules that hold for the vessels of the brain or muscles cannot automatically be transferred to the pulmonary circulation.

Breathing and blood pressure

Each inhalation and exhalation changes the pressure inside the chest, venous return, and the interaction between the heart and blood vessels. Slow or deep breathing may temporarily affect pulse rate and heart rate variability. But blood pressure is determined by many factors.

It is not possible to promise the same reduction in blood pressure to all people through breathing practice alone. With hypertension, heart rhythm disorders, and other cardiovascular diseases, breathing classes can be considered only as an addition to medical supervision.

The connection with the Buteyko method

In the Buteyko method, normalizing excessive ventilation is regarded as a way to eliminate an unnecessary fall in CO2 and the physiological reactions associated with it. It is correct to speak about the mechanism and about observing changes over time.
