The Bohr effect: how CO2 affects the release of oxygen to the tissues

Author: Alik Mullakhmetov, VEDB senior instructor and examiner, more than 30 years of practicing the Buteyko method

Updated: November 25, 2026

In short. When CO2 and acidity rise, hemoglobin holds oxygen less tightly and releases it to the tissues more easily. This does not mean that the more CO2, the better: oxygen delivery also depends on the lungs, circulation, and hemoglobin.

Hemoglobin carries oxygen

Most of the oxygen in the blood is carried by hemoglobin inside the red blood cells. In the lungs, where the partial pressure of oxygen is high, hemoglobin binds O2. In the tissues, oxygen separates and passes to the cells.

The ability of hemoglobin to bind oxygen is not constant. It changes depending on pH, CO2, temperature, and the concentration of 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate. This makes it possible to match oxygen release to the needs of the tissues.

What is called the Bohr effect

The Bohr effect is a decrease in the affinity of hemoglobin for oxygen when CO2 and the concentration of hydrogen ions rise. On a graph, this is described as a rightward shift of the oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve. At the same partial pressure of oxygen, hemoglobin holds it less tightly and releases it to the tissues more easily.

When CO2 falls and pH rises, the curve shifts to the left: affinity for oxygen increases. This may make its release in the periphery more difficult, although the oxygen saturation of hemoglobin in arterial blood may remain high.

What happens in working tissue

Active cells consume more oxygen and produce more CO2 and acidic products. Tissue temperature also rises. These local conditions help hemoglobin to release oxygen exactly where the need for it has increased.

Part of the CO2 enters the red blood cells and, with the help of carbonic anhydrase, is converted into bicarbonate and hydrogen ions. The hydrogen ions bind to hemoglobin and change its affinity for O2. CO2 itself can also form carbamino compounds with hemoglobin.

What happens in the lungs

In the alveoli there is more oxygen, and CO2 is removed. These conditions favor the binding of oxygen by hemoglobin. The saturation of hemoglobin with oxygen, in turn, makes it easier to release CO2; this is the Haldane effect, which is related to the Bohr effect but not identical to it.

Why the mechanism must not be oversimplified

It does not follow from the Bohr effect that high CO2 is always beneficial. An excessive rise in CO2 and acidosis disrupt the work of the body. Nor does the reverse conclusion follow: normal saturation does not guarantee ideal oxygen delivery to every tissue.

Oxygen delivery is determined by ventilation and diffusion in the lungs, hemoglobin content, cardiac output, blood flow in the particular organ, and the needs of the cells. The Bohr effect is an important link, but not the whole system.

The Bohr effect and hyperventilation

In hyperventilation, CO2 falls, pH rises, and the affinity of hemoglobin for oxygen increases. At the same time, hypocapnia may reduce blood flow in some organs, especially in the brain. Therefore, the effect of hyperventilation on the oxygen supply of tissues cannot be explained by saturation alone.

The connection with the Buteyko method

In materials about the Buteyko method, the Bohr effect helps to explain why CO2 is important for the use of oxygen by tissues. But a correct formulation should speak of the physiological range and the normalization of breathing, not of striving to raise CO2 at any cost.
