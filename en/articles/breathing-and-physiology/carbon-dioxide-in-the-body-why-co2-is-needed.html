Carbon dioxide in the body: why CO2 is needed

Author: Alik Mullakhmetov, VEDB senior instructor and examiner, more than 30 years of practicing the Buteyko method

Updated: November 25, 2026

In short. CO2 is not just a metabolic waste product: it takes part in the regulation of breathing, pH, vascular tone, and oxygen release to tissues. What matters for the body is neither to remove it completely nor to accumulate it without limit, but to maintain the physiological range.

Why CO2 cannot be considered only waste

Carbon dioxide is often seen only as a substance that must be removed from the body as quickly as possible. This is too simplistic. CO2 is indeed the end product of many metabolic processes, but at the same time it helps to maintain the constancy of the internal environment.

Both a lack and an excess of CO2 can disrupt the normal work of the body’s systems.

Where carbon dioxide is produced

Most CO2 is produced in the cells during the oxidation of nutrients. When the muscles, brain, and other organs use oxygen to obtain energy, one of the end products is carbon dioxide. The more intense the metabolism, the more CO2 is produced, so its production increases during physical exertion.

From the cells, CO2 passes into the tissue fluid and blood. A small part is carried in dissolved form, part binds to blood proteins, and the largest share is converted into bicarbonate. These transformations make it possible to carry carbon dioxide efficiently from the tissues to the lungs.

How CO2 is removed from the body

In the pulmonary capillaries, the reactions run in the opposite direction: CO2 is formed again from bicarbonate, passes into the alveoli, and is removed with exhalation. The rate of removal depends primarily on alveolar ventilation, the amount of fresh air that reaches the areas of the lungs involved in gas exchange in a minute.

If ventilation matches current metabolism, the CO2 content is maintained within the working range. If ventilation becomes excessive relative to CO2 production, CO2 falls. If ventilation is insufficient, it may rise.

The main functions of carbon dioxide

CO2 takes part in the bicarbonate buffer system and is therefore closely connected with blood pH. A change in ventilation can alter the amount of carbon dioxide quite quickly and shift the acid-base state.

Carbon dioxide also affects the smooth muscle of blood vessels. The vessels of the brain are especially sensitive to it: a fall in arterial CO2 usually reduces cerebral blood flow, while a rise increases it within certain physiological limits.

Another important connection is the Bohr effect
 Actively working tissues contain more CO2 and hydrogen ions. Under these conditions, the affinity of hemoglobin for oxygen decreases, and oxygen is released to the cells more easily. But this mechanism must not be turned into the formula “the more CO2, the better”: oxygen delivery also depends on the work of the lungs and heart, blood flow, the amount of hemoglobin, and the needs of the tissues.

CO2 and the regulation of breathing

The body constantly receives information about the chemical composition of the internal environment. Central and peripheral chemoreceptors take part in assessing changes in CO2, pH, and oxygen. For a healthy person under ordinary conditions, CO2 is one of the most important stimuli affecting ventilation.

This system works automatically, but breathing can at the same time change with speech, movement, emotions, and conscious control. Therefore, the breathing pattern is the result of the interaction of metabolism, nervous regulation, and behavior.

How the topic of CO2 is connected with the Buteyko method

In the methodological position of the Buteyko school, the goal of the practice is to normalize breathing and gas values, not to reduce breathing at any cost. If ventilation is excessive relative to the body’s needs, the work is aimed at gradually reducing its excess part, mainly through relaxation and elimination of the factors that deepen breathing.

An informational article cannot determine the CO2 level of a particular person. Medical methods are used for accurate assessment of blood gases, and any marked or new symptoms require ordinary clinical diagnosis.
