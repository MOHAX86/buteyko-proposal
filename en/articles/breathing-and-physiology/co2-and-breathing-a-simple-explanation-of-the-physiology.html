CO2 and breathing: a simple explanation of the physiology

Author: Alik Mullakhmetov, VEDB senior instructor and examiner, more than 30 years of practicing the Buteyko method

Updated: November 25, 2026

In short. The CO2 level depends on how much of the gas the cells produce and how much the lungs remove. Breathing is adequate to the situation when ventilation matches metabolism.

From the cells to the lungs

To understand how CO2 is connected with breathing, you need to picture a continuous cycle. The cells produce carbon dioxide, the blood carries it to the lungs, and ventilation removes it from the body. The CO2 level depends on the ratio of two processes: how much gas has been produced and how much has been removed.

At rest, metabolism is relatively stable, so the body needs comparatively little ventilation. During movement, the muscles produce more CO2, and ventilation naturally increases. The same volume of breathing can be normal during exertion and excessive at rest.

Minute and alveolar ventilation

Minute ventilation is the volume of air a person inhales or exhales in a minute. It depends on tidal volume and breathing rate. But not all of this air takes part in gas exchange.

Part of the air remains in the conducting airways: the nose, pharynx, trachea, and bronchi. This volume is called the anatomical dead space. For the exchange of O2 and CO2, alveolar ventilation is especially important: the volume of fresh air that reaches the alveoli in a minute.

That is why assessing breathing by rate alone is not enough. Infrequent but excessively deep breaths can create large ventilation. Conversely, rapid shallow breathing does not always mean effective renewal of alveolar air.

How ventilation affects CO2

With relatively constant CO2 production, an increase in alveolar ventilation lowers the alveolar and arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide. A decrease in ventilation acts in the opposite direction. This relationship underlies the physiological definition of hyperventilation.

It is important to take into account the condition “with the same CO2 production”. During exertion, the production of carbon dioxide increases, so heavier breathing may only be maintaining its usual level. Not every noticeable or deep breathing can be called hyperventilation without comparing it with metabolic demand.

Why a single breath does not give the full picture

Each breathing cycle renews the gas in the alveoli a little, but the alveoli do not empty completely. They always retain the functional residual capacity, a considerable volume of gas that smooths out fluctuations between inhalation and exhalation. At the same time, the blood continuously brings new CO2 to the lungs.

Therefore, the CO2 level cannot be reliably calculated by simply subtracting a fixed amount “per breath”. An accurate assessment requires data on ventilation, dead space, CO2 production, lung volume, and gas exchange.

What normalizing breathing means

Physiologically normal breathing is not a predetermined number of breaths, nor does it have to be outwardly imperceptible. It must provide gas exchange in accordance with the state of the body. The demand changes with exertion, temperature, fever, altitude, emotions, and diseases.

In the Buteyko method, attention is directed at the part of ventilation that is excessive. The depth of breathing is reduced to the physiological norm, mainly through relaxation rather than by forcibly restricting air.

Brief conclusion

What shows whether breathing is adequate to the situation is not the outward appearance of a single breath but how well ventilation matches current metabolism.
