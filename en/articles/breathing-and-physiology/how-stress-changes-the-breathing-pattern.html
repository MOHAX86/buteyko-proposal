How stress changes the breathing pattern

Author: Alik Mullakhmetov, VEDB senior instructor and examiner, more than 30 years of practicing the Buteyko method

Updated: November 25, 2026

In short. Under stress, breathing may become faster, deeper, or more uneven, and because of muscle tension it may feel restricted. Observation and relaxation are aimed at returning breathing to a calm state after stress.

The body prepares for action

The stress response helps to mobilize resources quickly. The autonomic nervous system and hormonal mechanisms are activated, and the pulse, muscle tone, attention, and breathing change. A short-term response is physiological in itself.

The problem arises when mobilization is excessive, repeats too often, or does not end in recovery. Then the breathing pattern may become fixed as habitual even in situations where increased ventilation is no longer needed.

How breathing changes

During agitation, breathing may become faster, deeper, shift to the upper chest, or become irregular. A person sighs more often, takes extra deep breaths, holds their breath while concentrating, and then compensates with a big inhalation.

The tone of the muscles of the neck, shoulders, chest, and abdomen increases. As a result, breathing may feel both heavier and restricted at the same time. Trying to “take a full breath” often increases ventilation even more.

When stress leads to hyperventilation

If ventilation becomes higher than required for the current CO2 production, the CO2 level falls. This is possible even without very rapid breathing: it is enough to steadily increase the depth of breaths or to sigh deeply often.

Outwardly, such a pattern can be barely noticeable. But observation alone is not enough to claim that chronic hyperventilation is present. Symptoms, circumstances, diseases, and, if necessary, objective data need to be taken into account.

How breathing intensifies sensations

A rapid fall in CO2 may cause dizziness, tingling, tightness in the chest, palpitations, and a feeling of air shortage. At the same time, muscle tension creates discomfort in the neck, chest, and abdomen.

If a person perceives these sensations as a threat, anxiety increases. They start watching every breath more closely and try to breathe more deeply. This is how a vicious circle forms: anxiety changes breathing, and changes in breathing intensify anxious sensations.

Compensation is not the same as health

The body is able to adapt partially to recurring changes. For example, with a prolonged fall in CO2, the kidneys change the way they handle bicarbonate. The person may feel the acute shift less.

But it cannot be concluded from this that any hidden hyperventilation necessarily causes hernias, osteochondrosis, or another specific disease. Such conditions have many risk factors. It is correct to speak of a possible additional load on the regulatory systems, while a causal link should be assessed separately.

How the Buteyko method works with this mechanism

The practice begins with observation: what happens to breathing during conversation, work, the news, conflict, and waiting. Then the person learns to remove unnecessary muscle tension and gradually reduce excessive depth of breathing to the physiological norm.

The work includes not only the exercise but also the factors that deepen breathing, meaning situations that make breathing deeper again. The goal is not to fight emotions but to return breathing to a calm state after stress.

When additional help is needed

New chest pain, fainting, marked shortness of breath, heart rhythm disturbances, and neurological symptoms must not be put down to stress. With prolonged anxiety, panic attacks, or disruption of everyday life, help from a doctor or a mental health specialist is useful. Breathing practice can complement such help but does not replace it.
