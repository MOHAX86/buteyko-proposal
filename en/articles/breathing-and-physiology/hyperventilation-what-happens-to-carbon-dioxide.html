Hyperventilation: what happens to carbon dioxide

Author: Alik Mullakhmetov, VEDB senior instructor and examiner, more than 30 years of practicing the Buteyko method

Updated: November 25, 2026

In short. In hyperventilation, the lungs remove CO2 faster than it is produced: its level falls, blood pH rises, and the blood vessels of the brain constrict.

What is hyperventilation

In physiology, hyperventilation is ventilation of the lungs that exceeds the body’s need to remove CO2 at a given metabolic rate. Its result is a decrease in alveolar and usually arterial CO2, called hypocapnia.

Hyperventilation is not the same as simply fast breathing. A person may breathe rapidly with small volumes, which does not cause a marked fall in CO2. Conversely, breathing may be infrequent but excessively deep. The main criterion is not how the breathing looks but how it relates to CO2 production.

Why CO2 falls

The blood constantly delivers carbon dioxide to the lungs. If alveolar ventilation increases sharply, CO2 is removed faster than it is produced in the tissues. Its partial pressure in the alveoli falls, and PaCO2 in arterial blood usually falls after it.

During physical work, CO2 production rises, so stronger ventilation may remain proportionate to the load. But if a person continues to breathe deeply after the exertion has stopped, or increases breathing at rest without a corresponding metabolic reason, hypocapnia is possible.

What changes hypocapnia triggers

CO2 is part of the bicarbonate buffer system. When there is less of it, the concentration of hydrogen ions decreases and pH rises. The rapid primary shift is called acute respiratory alkalosis.

A fall in CO2 also affects the blood vessels. The vessels of the brain constrict and cerebral blood flow decreases. At the same time, the change in pH raises the affinity of hemoglobin for oxygen, so its release to the tissues may become more difficult. These are different mechanisms, although they can act simultaneously.

How acute hyperventilation may manifest

A rapid fall in CO2 may cause dizziness, a sense of unreality, tingling around the mouth and in the fingers, muscle tension, palpitations, chest discomfort, and a feeling of air shortage.
Paradoxically, the feeling of “I can’t get a breath” may prompt a person to breathe even more deeply and lower CO2 further.

These symptoms are nonspecific. Similar sensations occur in diseases of the heart, lungs, and nervous system, in anemia, metabolic disorders, and other conditions. New, severe, or recurring symptoms cannot automatically be explained by hyperventilation.

Acute and prolonged hyperventilation

Acute hyperventilation is often noticeable. With a prolonged change in the breathing pattern, the outward signs may be less obvious: occasional deep breaths, frequent sighs, mouth breathing, tension of the breathing muscles, or a habitual increase in tidal volume.

With a prolonged fall in CO2, the kidneys change the way they handle bicarbonate and partially compensate for the shift in pH. Compensation reduces the degree of change in the acid-base state, but it does not mean that the original cause has been eliminated. At the same time, chronic hyperventilation cannot be established from a single complaint or outward observation.

Hyperventilation and the Buteyko method

In the Buteyko method, the term “deep breathing” is used to mean ventilation that is excessive relative to the body’s needs. The practice is aimed at gradually normalizing breathing, primarily through relaxation and work with the factors that deepen it.

The task is not to endure severe suffocation or to suppress normal ventilation. In case of marked discomfort, the exercise is stopped, and where there are diseases or unusual symptoms, the course of action is agreed with a doctor and an instructor.

Brief conclusion

The consequences of hyperventilation are connected with changes in pH, vascular tone, and the work of hemoglobin; however, symptoms require careful assessment and do not replace diagnosis.
