Body position during sleep and breathing

Author: Alik Mullakhmetov, VEDB senior instructor and examiner, more than 30 years of practicing the Buteyko method

Updated: November 25, 2026

In short. Body position can affect breathing during sleep, but a comfortable position does not need to be held by force. If apnea is suspected, position does not replace diagnosis and treatment.

Body position matters

During sleep a person changes position, and the position of the head, neck and trunk can affect how open the upper airways are. For some people with obstructive apnea, a doctor recommends sleeping on the side as part of treatment. This is not a universal way to eliminate any sleep problem. The chosen position should remain comfortable, especially with pain and limited mobility.

Why a general rule should not be given

For one person, lying on the back comes with louder snoring, while another notices no difference. The pillow, a blocked nose and accompanying illnesses also affect the sensation of breathing. A diagnosis cannot be made from the position. If family members notice pauses, choking or marked daytime sleepiness, a doctor is needed, not endless self-directed changes of position.

Comfort is more important than a strained position

Before sleep, check whether your head and shoulders are comfortably placed and whether congestion is interfering with nasal breathing. Do not try to hold a set position by force all night: a sleeping person naturally changes position. Excessive fixing of the body may itself interfere with falling asleep. In the Buteyko method, relaxation serves as a condition for working with excessive breathing, but it does not require strict control of the body during sleep.

With diagnosed apnea

If a doctor has prescribed CPAP or another form of treatment, body position may be only part of the plan. Do not stop the prescribed treatment because you managed to lie down more comfortably or do the evening practice. The decision on positional therapy depends on the results of the examination. Sleeping on the side may help some people, but it is not a guarantee of normal breathing all night.

What to observe

It is useful to note the quality of sleep, the condition of your nose and whether family members report snoring in different positions. There is no need to deliberately deprive yourself of sleep for an experiment. If night symptoms are significant, show your notes to a doctor. An instructor can help with daytime relaxation practice, but does not determine the degree of apnea from a description of your position.

What careful observation looks like

If sleeping on your side is more comfortable, write it down as a personal observation. There is no need to wake yourself all night to check your position or to set up uncomfortable barriers against turning over. Family members’ reports of snoring in different positions can add to the information for the doctor.
